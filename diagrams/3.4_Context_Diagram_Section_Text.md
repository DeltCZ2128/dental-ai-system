## 3.4 Context Diagram

A context diagram is the highest-level data flow diagram of a system. It
represents the whole system as a single process, numbered 0, and shows only
the external entities that exchange data with it and the data flows that move
between them. No internal processes, data stores, or files appear at this
level; those details are decomposed in the data flow diagrams of Section 3.5.
Every flow carries an arrowhead at its destination: a flow pointing into the
process is an input, and a flow pointing out of the process is an output.

### 3.4.1 Context Diagram of the Existing System

**Figure 3: Context Diagram of the Existing System**

Figure 3 presents the context diagram of the existing manual appointment
scheduling process of Abalos Dental Clinic. The single process, labelled 0,
stands for the entire manual workflow, and it exchanges data with three
external entities: PATIENT, CLINIC STAFF / FRONT DESK, and DENTIST.

The PATIENT initiates the workflow by submitting an appointment request
either as a walk-in or by phone, together with patient information such as
name, contact number, and dental concern. The same entity sends reschedule or
cancellation requests when the patient cannot attend, and service and fee
inquiries before booking. In return, the manual process issues the outputs
the patient takes home: an appointment confirmation written on an appointment
slip, a queue number for walk-in patients, an appointment reminder delivered
by call or text message, and a no-slot or conflict notice whenever the
requested schedule is unavailable.

The CLINIC STAFF / FRONT DESK entity feeds the process with the records it
maintains by hand: walk-in registration details, appointment logbook records,
dentist availability information such as rest days and day-offs, and service
and fee information given to patients. The process returns the working
documents the staff uses to run the clinic day to day: the daily appointment
list or schedule board, the patient record files, the monthly appointment
summary used for reporting, the no-show list of patients who failed to
attend, and the queue status of waiting patients.

The DENTIST provides working hours and availability, the treatment or service
duration used to size each appointment, the appointment status after each
visit (completed or no-show), and the treatment notes written during
consultation. The process returns the dentist's daily schedule and patient
list, the dental history of scheduled patients, and the confirmation details
of each booking.

Figure 3 therefore shows why the manual process is difficult to scale.
Appointments live in a logbook, dentist availability is confirmed verbally,
schedule conflicts and no-shows surface only after the fact, and there is no
automatic forecast of coming demand. These limitations motivate the proposed
system of the next section, in which the same three roles interact with a
centralized web-based system instead of with paper records.

### 3.4.2 Context Diagram of the Proposed System

**Figure 4: Context Diagram of the Proposed System**

Figure 4 presents the context diagram of the proposed AI-Based Intelligent
Dental Appointment Scheduling System Using Machine Learning for Optimized
Clinic Management. As in Figure 3, the whole system is drawn as one process,
numbered 0, and only the parties outside it and the data that crosses its
boundary are shown. The proposed system exchanges data with three external
entities, namely PATIENT, DENTIST, and ADMINISTRATOR. The figure documents
thirty-one data flows: sixteen flows enter the process as inputs and fifteen
flows leave it as outputs. Every flow carries a single arrowhead at its
destination, so a flow pointing into the process is an input and a flow
pointing out of it is an output.

The PATIENT supplies five of the inputs. Registration and login credentials
come first, because an account and a session are what allow the other patient
flows to be sent at all. Patient profile information follows, and then the
appointment request, which names the dentist the patient prefers, the service
to be rendered, and the date and time of the desired visit. Patients who
arrive without an account check in through the walk-in QR flow, and a patient
who can no longer attend sends an appointment concern that carries either a
reschedule or a cancel request. The process returns six outputs to the same
entity: the appointment confirmation together with its reference number, the
available schedule slots that the patient may still take, the appointment
notification and reminder, the appointment status, the queue number and queue
status issued to a patient who checks in on the day of the visit, and the
updated patient record. The appointment status is drawn from the five values
the system holds for an appointment, which are Pending, Confirmed, Completed,
Cancelled, and No-Show; a reschedule is not kept as a separate status but is
recorded as an appointment concern and announced to the patient as an
appointment rescheduled notification.

The DENTIST supplies five inputs as well. Login credentials open the session,
availability and working schedule describe the days and hours the dentist can
be booked, and rest days and day-offs block the dates on which no
consultation may be scheduled. Once a patient is in the chair, the dentist
sends the appointment status update and, after the consultation, the
treatment notes or medical record entry that become part of the patient's
clinical record. The process returns four outputs: the daily appointment
schedule together with the patient list of the day, the dental history and
medical records of the patients being seen, the appointment notifications
that concern the dentist, and the appointment reschedule concerns that
patients raise against booked visits.

The ADMINISTRATOR supplies six inputs, which are the maintenance data of the
clinic. User accounts and role management data record who may sign in and
with which role. Patient and dentist records hold the master data of the two
other parties. Dentist schedules, rest days, and day-offs keep the calendar
of each dentist consistent with the actual availability of the clinic.
Services and clinic settings hold the operating hours, the slot interval, and
the maximum number of patients per day that the scheduling logic must
respect. Appointment monitoring and confirmation cover the actions the
administrator takes on individual appointments, and concern resolution is the
reply sent back to a patient. The process returns five outputs: the dashboard
summary and key performance indicators, the appointment reports covering
volume, attendance, and no-show patterns, the seven-day demand forecast
classified as low, medium, or high, the queue monitoring information, and the
notification and system logs.

Two differences separate Figure 4 from Figure 3. The first is
centralization: because every party reads from and writes to the same
process, the administrator, the dentist, and the patient always act on the
same schedule data, the same appointment statuses, and the same queue, so a
schedule conflict or an unavailable dentist is reported while the appointment
is being booked instead of being discovered on the day of the visit. The
second is prediction: the process does more than record and report, because
it returns the seven-day demand forecast to the administrator so that the
clinic can plan its dentists and its slots ahead of the coming week. Section
3.5 decomposes this single process into the internal processes and data
stores that produce those flows.
